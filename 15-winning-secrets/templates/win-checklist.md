# Win Checklist

Use this in three phases: before demo, during demo, and after. Print it. Tape it to the wall. Check things off as you go.

---

## Pre-Demo Checklist (1 Hour Before)

### Setup
- [ ] Live demo URL works on a DIFFERENT device than the one you built on
- [ ] Tested demo on the actual venue WiFi (or your mobile hotspot as backup)
- [ ] Backup demo video loaded and ready to play (YouTube, not local file)
- [ ] 5+ screenshots saved locally on your device (not in the cloud)
- [ ] Printed one-page summary of your project (handout for judges)
- [ ] QR code for your repo/demo displayed on a slide
- [ ] All team members know the pitch timing
- [ ] Phone notifications silenced (airplane mode if presenting from it)

### Presentation
- [ ] Slides loaded and tested on the projector or TV
- [ ] Backup slides on USB drive AND in Google Drive
- [ ] Pitch rehearsed 3+ times with a timer
- [ ] Opening line memorized (you will blank — this saves you)
- [ ] Closing line memorized (this is what they remember most)
- [ ] All team members know their speaking parts
- [ ] Slide transitions work (test clicking through once)

### Demo Data
- [ ] Demo accounts created with pre-loaded data
- [ ] No real user data visible (privacy violation = instant disqualification)
- [ ] Edge cases covered: empty states, error states, success states
- [ ] Slow network plan in place
- [ ] All API keys removed from client-side code

### Team
- [ ] Everyone hydrated and fed
- [ ] Everyone knows the order: who speaks when
- [ ] Everyone has practiced their one-line role introduction
- [ ] Backup speaker designated (if someone gets sick or nervous)
- [ ] Everyone knows the emergency script

---

## During-Demo Checklist

### Opening (First 15 Seconds)
- [ ] Make eye contact with all judges before speaking
- [ ] Start with a question, fact, or story — NOT "Hi, we're..."
- [ ] State the problem in one sentence
- [ ] Show you understand who has this problem (specific user group)

### Problem (15-45 Seconds)
- [ ] Make the judges FEEL the pain (use a story or stat)
- [ ] Keep it under 30 seconds — don't lecture
- [ ] Transition cleanly: "That's why we built..."

### Solution (45-90 Seconds)
- [ ] Show, don't tell — get to the demo ASAP
- [ ] Name the product and explain it in one sentence
- [ ] Mention your tech stack in ONE line (don't list 8 frameworks)
- [ ] Highlight what makes it different from existing solutions

### Demo (90-150 Seconds)
- [ ] Walk through the core flow, not every feature
- [ ] Narrate what is happening on screen ("Watch this...")
- [ ] Point to the screen when showing key moments
- [ ] If demo fails, immediately switch to backup (don't apologize twice)
- [ ] Show at least one "wow moment" (something unexpected or clever)

### Impact (150-170 Seconds)
- [ ] State at least one concrete metric (users, speed, accuracy, improvement)
- [ ] Connect the metric to real-world impact (not just technical achievement)
- [ ] Mention any user testing or feedback you received

### Closing (170-180 Seconds)
- [ ] Restate the core value in one sentence
- [ ] End with a confident statement, not "that's it"
- [ ] Make eye contact with each judge
- [ ] Invite questions with a warm tone
- [ ] Hand out QR code or business card

### Q&A
- [ ] Answer what they asked, not what you wish they asked
- [ ] If you don't know: "That's a great question — we'd love to explore that next"
- [ ] Keep answers under 30 seconds
- [ ] Let other team members answer technical questions
- [ ] End with "Thank you" — not "that's all"

---

## Post-Demo Checklist

- [ ] Thank each judge by name (if you caught their name)
- [ ] Connect on LinkedIn with judges within 24 hours
- [ ] Tweet/post about the project with hackathon hashtag
- [ ] Push final code to GitHub with clean README
- [ ] Deploy the project (don't let it die after the event)
- [ ] Write a blog post or LinkedIn recap
- [ ] Send thank-you messages to teammates
- [ ] Save the project for your portfolio
- [ ] Note lessons learned while they're fresh (before your next hackathon)

## Judge Eye Contact Guide

This sounds trivial but it matters more than you think.

**The Pattern:**
- When stating the problem → look at Judge A (left)
- When revealing the solution → look at Judge B (center)
- When showing the demo → point to screen, glance at Judge C (right)
- When stating impact → look at all three, slow sweep left to right
- When closing → look at the judge who seems most engaged

**What NOT to do:**
- Don't stare at one judge the whole time. It feels aggressive.
- Don't read from notes. Glance, don't stare.
- Don't look at the screen for more than 3 seconds without looking back at the judges.
- Don't look at your teammates during the pitch (it signals uncertainty).

**The Confidence Hack:**
If you're nervous, look at the judge who is smiling or nodding. Their positive energy feeds you. Save the serious-looking judge for last — by then you'll be warmed up.

## Recovery Moves When Things Go Wrong

### "The demo just crashed"
> Pause for 2 seconds. Then: "Technology keeps us humble. Here's a 30-second clip of it working perfectly." Play backup video. Never apologize more than once.

### "I forgot my next line"
> Take a breath. Say: "Let me restate that." Then go back to your last clear point and continue. Nobody knows your script except you.

### "A judge asked a question I can't answer"
> "That's a great question. We didn't get to that in the build, but here's how I'd approach it..." Then give a 15-second thoughtful answer. Never say "I don't know" without following up.

### "Our demo is slower than expected"
> "Let me walk you through the flow while it loads." Then describe what the user is seeing. Turn a delay into a storytelling opportunity.

### "My teammate is talking too long"
> Gently step in: "And to add to that —" then take over with your part. Judges won't notice the transition if you're smooth about it.

### "The WiFi just died"
> Switch to your mobile hotspot immediately. If that fails too: "Let me show you the architecture and screenshots instead." Pull up your backup. Don't restart the WiFi — it wastes precious seconds.

### "A judge looks bored or is checking their phone"
> Move closer to them physically. Ask them a direct question: "Have you ever experienced this problem yourself?" Engagement is a two-way street.

## Scoring Self-Assessment

Rate yourself honestly after your last practice run (1-5 scale).

### Problem Clarity
- Could someone who knows nothing about your project understand the problem in 10 seconds?
- Did you explain WHO has this problem, not just WHAT the problem is?
- Score: ___ / 5

### Solution Simplicity
- Can you explain your solution in one sentence?
- Did you avoid technical jargon that judges won't understand?
- Score: ___ / 5

### Demo Effectiveness
- Does the demo work 9 out of 10 times?
- Is there a "wow moment" that surprises even you?
- Does the demo flow feel natural, not mechanical?
- Score: ___ / 5

### Impact Evidence
- Do you have at least one concrete number (users, speed, accuracy)?
- Can you connect that number to real human impact?
- Did you mention any user feedback or testing?
- Score: ___ / 5

### Pitch Delivery
- Did you stay within the time limit?
- Did you make eye contact with all judges?
- Did you speak clearly without rushing?
- Did you end with confidence, not trailing off?
- Score: ___ / 5

### Q&A Readiness
- Can you answer 5 common questions without hesitation?
- Do you have a go-to phrase for questions you can't answer?
- Did you let teammates handle questions in their expertise area?
- Score: ___ / 5

### Total Score: ___ / 30

| Score | Rating |
|-------|--------|
| 26-30 | Competition-ready. Focus on polish. |
| 21-25 | Strong foundation. Practice more. |
| 16-20 | Work to do. Prioritize demo reliability. |
| 11-15 | Rebuild your pitch from scratch. |
| 5-10 | Start over. You're not ready yet. |
